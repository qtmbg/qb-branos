# QB BrandOS Voice Codex v1
## How Quantum Branding speaks. Across every surface, every tool, every interaction.
### Drop-in for Claude Code, writers, agents, AI prompts. Synced with QB Thinking Machine Master Instruction v4 and Design System v3.4 (April 2026).

This document locks the QB written voice the same way v3.4 locks the visuals. Every word a user reads inside the QB BrandOS ecosystem flows through this codex. Marketing pages, in-tool copy, AI agent responses, button labels, error messages, empty states, emails, social posts. One voice. Locked.

The codex exists for one reason: voice drift is the fastest way for a brand to feel cheap. A site can have a perfect design system and still read like a SaaS dashboard if the words are off. QB has a precise visual identity. The writing has to keep up.

---

## Part 1: The Voice in One Page

QB sounds like a thoughtful founder, not a software company.

Direct. Calm. Slightly contrarian. Aware of the weight of the work. Refuses jargon. Refuses hype. Trusts the reader.

A useful test: if a sentence could appear on a $79 SaaS landing page, it does not belong in QB. If a sentence could appear in a serious essay about how brands actually get built, it does.

Three voice anchors:

**Aphoristic.** Short declarative claims that hold weight. "Idea in, brand out." "Three steps. That's it." "Twenty tools, one brain." Sentences earn their place by being unforgettable, not by being thorough.

**Confident without strain.** No exclamation points. No urgency manufactured by countdown timers, "limited offer" framing, or fake scarcity. The product is good. The reader is intelligent. Both facts are assumed. Neither is announced.

**Editorial, not promotional.** The reader is reading a magazine, not a pitch deck. Sections build narratively. Headlines feel written, not engineered. Body copy explains rather than persuades. Persuasion is a side effect of clarity, not the goal.

If you want the test in three sentences:

1. Would a thoughtful person quote this on LinkedIn? Then it lives.
2. Would a tired founder roll their eyes at this? Then it dies.
3. Would this sentence still work if every other brand in the category disappeared overnight? Then it earns rent.

## Part 2: Voice Anchors (full)

These are the five qualities every QB sentence carries. They are non-negotiable. A sentence that fails any of them fails the brand.

### 2.1 Direct

The QB reader has built things, lost things, rebuilt things. They are out of patience for warm-up sentences. Get to the point in the first clause.

Direct does not mean blunt. It means the structural decision to put the most important word as close to the start of the sentence as possible. Put the verb to work. Put the noun in front.

Tells of indirectness:
- "We believe that..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- "What we've found is that..."
- "There's a question we get asked a lot..."

Each one of those phrases is the writer clearing their throat in public. Cut them. Start with the actual thing.

### 2.2 Calm

QB does not panic the reader into action. Other software brands in the category lean on adrenaline ("Don't get left behind!" "Your competitors are already using AI!"). QB never does. The reader is not a deer to be startled into a fence. The reader is a peer who is trying to make a serious decision and would like to be treated as such.

Calm shows up as:
- No exclamation points outside of literal user-facing celebration moments (e.g., "Saved." after a save action). Even those are rare.
- No countdown timers, blinking indicators, or "act now" framing.
- No emoji in product copy. None. In marketing copy, almost never.
- No interrupting modal popups asking for the email twice.

If a sentence sounds like it was written at 2 a.m. by someone running on caffeine, rewrite it.

### 2.3 Slightly contrarian

QB has a point of view. Inside the brand methodology there are arguments: identity before tactics, structure before expression, transformation before appearance. These show up in the writing as small, well-placed disagreements with the default way founders think about branding.

Examples of the contrarian pulse:

- A typical SaaS would write "Build your dream brand in 5 minutes."
- QB writes "A brand isn't built in five minutes. The diagnostic is."

- A typical SaaS would write "AI-powered branding that scales with you."
- QB writes "Twenty agents that all read from one Brand Profile. Nothing drifts. Everything compounds."

- A typical SaaS would write "Designed for solopreneurs and agencies."
- QB writes "Built for the founder with one idea and zero assets."

The contrarian instinct is not about being negative. It is about refusing the lazy shorthand that other brands in the category have settled for. Every time you reach for an industry phrase, ask whether QB believes it or just inherited it.

### 2.4 Aware of weight

The work the user is doing inside QB is real. They are deciding how their company sounds, what it stands for, who it serves, how it shows up in the world. That is identity-level work. Treating it as a click-through funnel demeans them.

Aware of weight shows up as:
- Methodological language used carefully. The four Phase 01 tools (Brand Soul Map, Sensescape, Visual DNA, War Table) are described as diagnostic instruments, not as quizzes or worksheets. The Brand Profile is described as a data spine, not as a profile picture.
- Refusal to oversimplify. If a step is hard, the copy says it is hard.
- Refusal to over-celebrate. If the user finishes Phase 01, the system says "Brand Profile complete. Ready for Phase 02." It does not say "🎉 Amazing work! You're a brand wizard!"

### 2.5 Trusts the reader

QB does not over-explain, over-handhold, or over-disclaim. The reader is treated as someone who can read a sentence once and understand it. Repeating the value proposition three times in three different ways is a tell that the writer didn't trust the first version.

Trust shows up as:
- Short paragraphs.
- Sentences that end on the strong word.
- No "as we mentioned earlier" callbacks.
- No "in summary" or "in conclusion" wrap-ups.
- No "(see what I mean?)" or "(pretty cool, right?)" winks.

The reader does not need the writer's approval. The writer should not need the reader's.

## Part 3: Mechanics (the line-level rules)

Voice anchors are the soul. Mechanics are the tendons. Every QB sentence respects these.

### 3.1 Punctuation

**No em dashes.** Use periods, semicolons, or commas. The em dash is the AI tell of the moment. It also tends to bloat sentences that wanted to be two sentences. Cut it everywhere. If you find yourself reaching for one, the sentence wants to break in half.

Wrong: "The Brand Profile is the spine of the system — every tool reads from it."

Right: "The Brand Profile is the spine of the system. Every tool reads from it."

**No semicolons in marketing copy.** They feel academic and slow the cadence. Semicolons are fine in long-form essays or technical documentation. They die on a landing page.

**Commas, used sparingly.** QB sentences are short enough that comma-heavy construction is rarely needed. If a sentence has three commas, it probably wants to be two sentences.

**Periods on sentence fragments are allowed and encouraged.** "Three steps. That's it." reads better than "Three steps; that is all there is." Aphorism over grammar.

**No exclamation points.** Outside of literal celebration moments inside the product (rare). Marketing copy never uses them.

**Quotation marks are for actual quotations.** Not for emphasis, not for irony, not for hedging. If a word is "controversial" you don't trust it. Cut the quotes or cut the word.

**Apostrophes are straight or curly, consistent across the file.** Whichever the platform renders better.

### 3.2 Casing

**Sentence case for headlines.** "Idea in, brand out" not "Idea In, Brand Out". Sentence case reads warmer and more editorial. Title case reads like a press release.

**The wordmark is always lowercase italic Fraunces** (per Design System v3.4 Part 21.5). Even at the start of a sentence. Even in headlines.

**The product name capitalizes inconsistently on purpose:**
- "Quantum Branding" is the company name, in running prose. Capitalized.
- "QB BrandOS" is the product. Always exactly this casing. Never "QB Brandos," "QB Brand OS," or "qb brandos."
- "quantum branding" is the wordmark glyph (italic Fraunces). Always lowercase.

**Phase names** are capitalized: "Phase 01 Discovery," "Phase 02 Brand Creation." When the phase is referenced casually in body copy, "phase one" or "the discovery phase" lowercase is fine.

**Tool names** are capitalized as proper nouns: Signal Scan, Brand Soul Map, Sensescape, Visual DNA, War Table, Logo Direction, Voice Guide, Predictive Panel, Quarterly Brand Review, Brand Profile.

### 3.3 Sentence length

Average target: 8 to 14 words per sentence. The codex itself runs longer because it is reference documentation. User-facing copy should run shorter.

A few longer sentences for breath are fine. Three long sentences in a row is a problem. Reading rhythm matters.

### 3.4 Paragraph length

Two to four sentences per paragraph in marketing copy. Single-sentence paragraphs are allowed and powerful when used sparingly. Five-sentence paragraphs in body copy almost always want to be split.

### 3.5 Pronouns

**"You" is the user.** Always direct address. Never "users" or "founders" in third person when the same sentence could speak to the reader.

Wrong: "Founders need a system that scales with them."

Right: "You need a system that holds together as you grow."

**"We" is Quantum Branding the company.** Used sparingly. The voice is more often the product speaking ("the system walks you through identity") than the company speaking ("we built this system to walk you through identity"). The company recedes. The work speaks.

**"I" is the founder.** Used only in the founder block, in newsletters from Nizzar, and in social posts attributed to him personally. Never elsewhere.

### 3.6 Numbers

**Numerals for metrics, units, and pricing.** $97. 20 agents. 6 phases. 5 minutes. 14-day money-back.

**Words for small counts inside running prose.** "Three steps. That's it." "One Brand Profile, every tool reads it."

The split feels arbitrary. It is. The rule is what feels right at reading speed. Six tools = numeral when listed, "six tools" when written into a sentence's rhythm.

### 3.7 Emphasis

**Italics carry the spotlight phrase** (per Design System v3.4, italic gold for hero headlines). In body copy, italics for occasional emphasis are fine. Never italicize a whole paragraph.

**Bold for genuine emphasis only.** Not for keywords. Not for SEO. Not for "make the eye stop here." If three bolds appear in one paragraph, none of them are bold anymore.

**No underlining outside hyperlinks.** Underlining a word for emphasis reads as 1998.

**No ALL CAPS for emphasis.** Mono caps tags (per Design System v3.4 Part 9.2) are a typographic element, not an emphasis device.

### 3.8 Contractions

Allowed and encouraged. "Don't" reads warmer than "do not." QB is a thinking founder, not a corporate disclaimer. "It's," "you're," "we've" all good. The exception is legal copy and terms documents, where formality serves the reader.

## Part 4: Word List (in / out)

### 4.1 Words and phrases that live

These belong in QB writing. Use them, not as crutches, but as available material.

- Brand. Identity. Voice. Coherence. Truth. Resonance. Position. Story. Soul. Signal. Diagnostic. Compass. Map. Profile.
- Connected. Compounding. Holding together. Reads from. Feeds into. Doesn't drift. Stays consistent.
- Built for. Designed around. Tuned to. Calibrated for. Anchored in.
- Phase. Tool. Agent. System. Operating system.
- Founder. Builder. Operator. Maker. Creator. Leader.
- Methodology. Philosophy. Practice. Discipline.

### 4.2 Words and phrases that die

These are banned from QB writing. They mark the brand as belonging to the same lazy SaaS layer QB is differentiating from.

**Banned outright:**
- Empower. Unlock. Supercharge. Boost. Transform (used loosely). Revolutionize. Disrupt. Game-changer. Cutting-edge. Best-in-class. World-class.
- Seamless. Frictionless. Effortless. Robust. Scalable (when used as a vague positive).
- Solutions. Synergy. Leverage (as a verb). Optimize. Streamline. Maximize.
- Journey (when describing the user's path through the product. The product has phases, not journeys. The user has work, not journeys).
- "AI-powered." "AI-driven." "AI-native." Cliché. If AI is relevant, name what it does.
- "Take your brand to the next level." "Stand out from the crowd." "Make your mark."
- "Welcome to the future of [X]."
- "Are you tired of [thing]?" Direct-response opener. Banned.
- "What if I told you..." Banned.

**Banned as filler:**
- Really, very, just, literally, actually, basically, simply, truly. Most of these are crutches the sentence works without. Cut and reread.
- "In order to" → "to."
- "At the end of the day" → cut.
- "Moving forward" → cut.
- "It goes without saying" → if it goes without saying, do not say it.

**Banned as the wrong register:**
- "Hey there!" "Hi friends!" "Folks." Too casual.
- "We're thrilled / excited / delighted." Cool the temperature down.
- "Pro tip." "Hot take." "Real talk." Performative casualness.

### 4.3 Words to handle with care

These are not banned. They are easy to abuse. Use only when the sentence cannot land without them.

- "Powerful." Often empty. If something is powerful, show what it does, don't claim the adjective.
- "Smart." Same problem. The system is smart. Show how.
- "Beautiful." Allowed for design moments. Banned for everything else.
- "Effortless." Borderline banned. The work QB does is hard. Pretending otherwise insults the user.
- "Curated." Almost always pretentious. Use only when something has actually been curated by a human.
- "Honestly." If a sentence needs "honestly" in front, the rest is suspect.
- "Quantum." Yes, the brand is named Quantum Branding. The word "quantum" outside the brand name is banned. No "quantum leap," "quantum thinking," "quantum-powered." The name does the work.

## Part 5: Surface-by-surface guide

The voice anchors and mechanics are the system. This section is the application layer: how the voice expresses on each specific surface inside QB.

### 5.1 Marketing pages (index, ecosystem, tools, pricing)

**Tone:** editorial, slightly aphoristic, confident without strain. The reader is reading a magazine about a serious product.

**Headlines:** Fraunces italic. Sentence case. Often two-clause: declarative claim + italic spotlight phrase. "Idea in, brand out." "Three steps. That's it." "Before you decide."

**Body paragraphs:** two to four sentences, average 8-14 words per sentence. Each section earns its scroll (per Design System v3.4 Part 20.8).

**CTAs:** verb-led, clear stakes, no manufactured urgency.
- Right: "Start free." "Run your Signal Scan." "See how it works."
- Wrong: "Get started today!" "Claim your free scan!" "Don't miss out!"

**Subheads under headlines:** explain in one sentence, not three. The subhead does not restate the headline. It clarifies it.

**Pricing page:** clean numerals, one-line tier descriptions, no "billed annually for $X.XX/year (just $Y.YY/month!)" math gymnastics. The reader can calculate. Tier names are sentence case ("Brand Profile, Starter, Pro, Agency, Enterprise"). Feature lists are scanable, not sales-led.

### 5.2 Banner copy

The banner is the same line on every page until further notice (per Master Instruction Lock). Verbatim:

"Signal Scan is live. Free brand diagnostic. 5 minutes to your first insight. Run yours →"

Do not paraphrase. Do not "improve" the wording per page. The banner is a system constant.

### 5.3 Inside the tools (Phase 01-05 agents)

**Tone:** the system is a guide, not a host. It speaks like a careful diagnostic, not like a chatbot trying to be friendly.

**Tool intros:** one or two sentences explaining what this tool does and what the user will walk out with. Not what the tool is technically. What changes for the user.

Right: "Brand Soul Map surfaces the core truth of your brand. You walk out with the one-line essence that sits underneath every other decision."

Wrong: "Welcome to Brand Soul Map! 🎉 This tool uses advanced AI to help you discover your brand essence. Let's get started!"

**Question prompts:** the questions inside Phase 01 tools are diagnostic instruments. They are written carefully, not casually.

Right: "What is the core promise your brand makes that, if broken, would break the brand entirely?"

Wrong: "What's something special about your brand?"

**System messages:** terse, useful, never apologetic.

Right: "Saved."

Right: "Brand Profile updated. Voice Guide is reading from it."

Wrong: "Awesome! We've saved your work! 🎉"

**Loading and processing states:** name what the system is doing if it takes more than two seconds.

Right: "Reading your Brand Profile..."

Right: "Drafting in your voice..."

Wrong: "Hang tight! Magic is happening..."

**AI agent responses (the tools that call Claude):** the agent speaks as the QB system, not as Claude. The agent is direct, methodical, and refuses to be cute. If the agent has a recommendation, it states it plainly. If the agent has a doubt, it names the doubt.

Right: "Voice Guide recommends shifting to first-person storytelling. Sensescape suggests warmer tones. I have drafted twelve posts in this direction. Approve, revise, or restart."

Wrong: "Hey! So I was thinking, maybe you could try first-person? It might work better! Let me know what you think 😊"

The agent is a specialist consultant, not a customer service rep.

### 5.4 Empty states

Empty states are written, not improvised. Each one carries the same shape: brief acknowledgment of the empty state, one sentence of what to do next, the action.

Right: "No agents have run yet. Start with Signal Scan to populate your Brand Profile. [Start free]"

Wrong: "Looks like there's nothing here yet! Why not get started by running your first agent? It's super easy! [Get Started]"

Empty states never apologize for being empty. They never use "oops" or "uh oh." They state the situation and the next step.

### 5.5 Error states

Errors are written as concise diagnostics. Name what failed in language the user can act on. Never use a stack trace as user-facing copy. Never blame the user.

Right: "We couldn't reach the AI service. Try again in a moment, or check your connection."

Right: "This Brand Profile is missing required fields. Complete Phase 01 to continue."

Wrong: "Oh no! Something went wrong! 😱 Please try again or contact support if the problem persists."

Wrong: "Error 403: Forbidden."

Errors are the moment users decide whether your product is professional or amateur. Treat them with the same care as the hero copy.

### 5.6 Email (transactional and newsletter)

**Transactional emails** (welcome, payment confirmation, password reset, weekly digest):
- Subject line: factual, no emoji, no urgency. "Your Brand Profile is ready." "Payment confirmed." "This week in your QB BrandOS."
- Greeting: first name only, no exclamation. "Hi [name],"
- Body: short. Three to five sentences max. State what happened. State what is now possible. Link to the next step.
- Sign-off: "Quantum Branding" or "Nizzar" depending on context. No "Cheers!" "Best!" "Take care!" with exclamation points.

**Newsletter emails (from Nizzar):**
- First-person voice. Personal, not corporate.
- Subject line: a thought, a claim, or a fragment. "What identity is built on." "Why most brands sound the same." Not "📰 Newsletter #42 — Branding Tips Inside!"
- Length: 200-500 words typically. Long enough to land an idea. Short enough to read in two minutes.
- Tone: thinking aloud about the work, not promoting the product. The product gets one mention near the end, framed as the natural next step for someone interested in the thinking.

### 5.7 Social posts (LinkedIn, X, Instagram captions)

**LinkedIn (the primary channel):**
- Posts are essays in compressed form. 4 to 12 sentences. Strong opening line.
- No "I'm thrilled to announce..." starts. No "P.S." chains. No "Agree?" hooks at the end.
- Posts state a position, defend it briefly, end on the strong sentence.
- The brand voice and the founder voice converge here. Nizzar writes in first person. The position belongs to him personally and to QB by extension.

**X (less frequent):**
- Short. One thought. No threads of 18 tweets.
- Same voice anchors apply but compressed.
- Quote-tweets and replies are fine. Subtweets and arguments are not.

**Instagram captions:**
- One to three sentences accompanying a visual.
- The caption does not narrate the image. The caption adds a thought the image alone could not carry.
- No hashtag chains. Three or fewer hashtags if any, all lowercase, all relevant.

### 5.8 White-label client output

When the QB system generates content for an Agency-tier client, the voice flexes to match the client's brand. The QB voice anchors stay intact under the hood (calm, direct, aware of weight) but the surface vocabulary, sentence rhythm, and reference frames shift to fit the client.

The system does this by reading from the client's Brand Profile and Voice Guide outputs. The QB voice is the chassis. The client's voice is the paint.

What never flexes: the refusal to use AI-tells, exclamation chains, em dashes, banned phrases (per 4.2). Even in the client's voice, those are out. QB ships writing that holds up at any client level.

## Part 6: The Personas (how voice flexes by audience)

The four doors (per Master Instruction v4) hit the product at different stages with different mental states. Voice flexes slightly to meet each.

### 6.1 The Blank Slate (Door 01, "I have an idea, no brand yet")

**State of mind:** excited, uncertain, slightly intimidated by the work ahead. Has not done branding before. Might feel that "branding" is for bigger companies.

**Voice flex:** more reassuring on the front edge, more methodical through the work. Avoid jargon. Define terms as they appear. The first interaction explains what a Brand Profile is in one sentence the first time the term shows up.

**What dies:** assumptions about prior work. Phrases like "as you already know" or "since you've done this before."

**Sample tone:**

"You start at the beginning. The system walks you through four diagnostic tools. You answer questions about what you stand for, who you serve, how the brand should feel and sound. By the end of Phase 01, you have a Brand Profile. The Brand Profile is the spine that every other tool reads from."

### 6.2 The Doubter (Door 02, "I have a brand, something feels off")

**State of mind:** frustrated, slightly defensive about the work already done, looking for a clear diagnostic before they invest more time. Might suspect the existing brand is salvageable, might suspect a rebuild.

**Voice flex:** sharper, more diagnostic. The Doubter does not need explanation. The Doubter needs to feel that QB will tell them the truth about what they have.

**What dies:** softness. Phrases like "every brand is unique and beautiful in its own way." Banned for the Doubter. The Doubter wants verdicts.

**Sample tone:**

"You don't need to start over. You need to know what's holding. The diagnostic tools audit your current identity, voice, and visual language. They tell you what to keep, what to fix, and what to rebuild. The result is a precise verdict, not a vague vibe."

### 6.3 The Player (Door 03, "Competition is coming fast")

**State of mind:** moving quickly, wants leverage, wants to skip ahead to the strategy and intelligence layers. Already has the basics. Needs to know if QB can keep up with their pace.

**Voice flex:** tighter, more strategic, less philosophical. The Player is action-oriented. The voice meets them at action speed.

**What dies:** long methodology paragraphs. The Player will skim them. Open with the action.

**Sample tone:**

"Skip to strategy. The War Table pressure-tests your next move. The Predictive Panel simulates market response before you spend on the launch. The Quarterly Brand Review tells you what worked, what didn't, and what to scale next."

### 6.4 The Multi-Brand (Door 04, "I build for clients")

**State of mind:** professional, evaluating QB as infrastructure for their agency. Cares about white-label fidelity, multi-tenant logic, output consistency across clients.

**Voice flex:** peer-to-peer. The voice acknowledges that the Multi-Brand is also in the brand business. No condescension, no over-explanation.

**What dies:** founder-onboarding language. The Multi-Brand does not need to be told what a Brand Profile is.

**Sample tone:**

"Up to five client workspaces. White-label per client. Multi-tenant Brand Profiles. The agency runs each client through the QB methodology under your branding. Your logo, your colors, the QB system underneath."

## Part 7: Voice Tests

Before publishing any QB copy, run it through these. If a piece fails one, rewrite. If it fails two, scrap and start over.

### 7.1 The em dash test

Search the document for em dashes. If any are present, the document fails. Replace with periods, commas, or sentence breaks.

### 7.2 The banned phrase test

Search for: empower, unlock, supercharge, seamless, solutions, leverage (verb), journey (as user path), AI-powered, take your brand to the next level, stand out from the crowd, in order to, at the end of the day, world-class, best-in-class, cutting-edge, robust, frictionless, effortless (when overused), really, very, just, literally.

If any are present, rewrite. The presence of even one is a tell that the writer was on autopilot.

### 7.3 The exclamation point test

Search for "!" in the document. Count occurrences. If more than zero in marketing copy, scrutinize each one. Inside-product celebration moments may have one. Outside, none.

### 7.4 The first-line test

Read the first sentence aloud. Does it earn the reader's attention? Or does it warm up the reader for the real first sentence three lines down? If the latter, cut the warm-up.

### 7.5 The "would Nizzar say this" test

If a sentence sounds like nobody at the company would actually say it out loud at a coffee shop, it dies. The voice is the founder's thinking expressed at a polished register. Not corporate-speak. Not chatbot-speak.

### 7.6 The remove-the-product test

Read the copy with all mentions of QB BrandOS, the agents, and the tool names removed. Does the remaining writing still hold a thought? If yes, the writing is doing its job. If no, the writing is just product description with verbs.

### 7.7 The competitor swap test

Replace "Quantum Branding" or "QB BrandOS" with a competitor's name (Brandcrowd, Looka, Canva). Does the copy still make sense? If yes, the copy is generic to the category. Rewrite until the copy is specific enough that swapping the brand name breaks the meaning.

### 7.8 The "would I quote this" test

Could a thoughtful reader screenshot a sentence and post it without context as something worth thinking about? If at least one sentence per major piece passes this test, the writing is working.

## Part 9: When voice flexes, when it doesn't

The voice anchors (Part 2) never flex. The mechanics (Part 3) flex slightly by surface (newsletter is more personal, error messages are terser). The vocabulary (Part 4) is locked.

Surfaces where voice flexes most:
- Newsletter from the founder (more personal, first person)
- Social posts (more compressed, sometimes more provocative)
- White-label client output (paint changes, chassis stays)

Surfaces where voice flexes least:
- Marketing pages (full editorial register)
- Pricing page (factual, restrained)
- Inside-tool agent responses (specialist consultant register)
- Error and empty states (terse, useful, never apologetic)

If a piece of writing feels off-voice, it is almost always one of three things. An em dash slipped in. A banned phrase from 4.2 made it through. The first sentence is warming up instead of starting.

These three are the most common failure modes. They are how voice drift first appears.
